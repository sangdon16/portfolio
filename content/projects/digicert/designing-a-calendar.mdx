        <p>The project began with a reference prompt shared by someone from another marketing department with Marcelo, my Senior Manager, during the 2026 Sales Kick-off.</p>

    <CampaignSection id="requirements" toc="Shared requirements" title="A shared pattern needed to handle very different calendars">
      <Copy>
        <p>A reusable pattern could not depend on milestones arriving at regular intervals.</p>
        <p>Some periods could contain several changes. Others might contain none. A long-term PQC view could span years, while another use case might focus on a single month.</p>
        <p>The information also needed to remain digestible. Showing more dates or more controls would not necessarily help someone understand what mattered.</p>
        <p>Across the reviews, the requirement became clearer. Present the relevant information compactly without making the reader work through unnecessary structure.</p>
      </Copy>
      <Lens kind="question" title="How could one pattern accommodate different calendar needs while keeping the information easy to understand?" />
    </CampaignSection>

    <CampaignSection id="timeline" toc="Timeline" title="Make the passage of time visible">
      <Copy>
        <p>The initial timeline direction organized milestones against a shared time structure.</p>
        <p>This offered a useful way to see when events occurred relative to one another. The swimlane exploration developed that idea through columns containing milestone cards.</p>
        <p>I explored different amounts of visible time and how opening an event detail panel affected the available space. More visible columns supported a broader overview. Fewer columns gave individual entries more room.</p>
        <p>The challenge was that the structure reserved space for time whether or not there was a milestone to show.</p>
        <p>With unevenly distributed content, some columns could be crowded while others remained empty. Adjusting the number of visible columns changed the density. It did not remove that underlying tension.</p>
      </Copy>

    <CampaignSection id="narrowing" toc="Narrowing the options" title="More visible time did not necessarily mean more useful information">
      <Copy>
        <p>The two-month and early summary/grid directions were the first options eliminated during these reviews.</p>
        <p>The concern was that readers would encounter blank months or overview structure before reaching useful information. That worked against the goal of a compact, digestible experience.</p>
        <p>For the calendar direction, the preference was one month at a time with navigation, rather than showing several months simultaneously.</p>
        <p>This narrowed the exploration while preserving different ways of accessing the content. The single-month calendar still supported date-based browsing, while the agenda offered a more direct way to scan entries.</p>
        <p>The early summary/grid exploration is not the same as every later design that uses the word grid. A later card-based Grid, or Monthly Summary, was a separate candidate and is called out on its own where it appears.</p>
      </Copy>
      <Lens kind="tradeoff" title="Reducing the visible time range creates a more focused view, but requires navigation to explore other periods." />

    <CampaignSection id="decision" toc="Stakeholder decision" title="Choose the agenda for unevenly distributed milestones">
      <Copy>
        <p>At the stakeholder meeting, we agreed that the agenda was the right direction.</p>
        <p>Atri, our Chief Marketing Officer, highlighted the problem with the swimlane approach. When there were no milestones for several months or years, those time columns would remain empty.</p>
        <p>I shared that concern. For this content, the empty structure could occupy attention without helping someone understand the next relevant change.</p>
        <p>The agenda alternative I had prepared grouped entries by months with changes. This preserved chronological order without reserving equal space for inactive periods.</p>
        <p>The agreement was to prioritize the information people needed to review over a continuous visual representation of time.</p>
        <p>This was a stakeholder-supported design decision based on the content and its presentation. A user study did not establish the agenda’s superiority.</p>
      </Copy>

    <CampaignSection id="actions" toc="Action hierarchy" title="Help readers understand what a change means for them">
      <Copy>
        <p>The selected agenda direction does more than list dates.</p>
        <p>Each entry brings together the change, its timing, the action a reader may need to take, and supporting information. Some entries explicitly state that no action is required. Others describe a task or a conditional response.</p>
        <p>This distinction matters because learning that something is changing does not automatically explain how to respond.</p>
        <p>The layout also separates the main entry date from the enforcement-date information. That gives the design room to communicate more than one relevant date without treating them as interchangeable.</p>
        <p>Supporting resources sit behind “Learn more” controls, allowing the main entry to foreground the change and its practical consequence.</p>
        <p>The titles and dates below are example design content from the selected screen. They are not an independent reading of the underlying requirements.</p>
      </Copy>

      <Copy>
        <p>The agenda direction also has a mobile presentation.</p>
        <p>The desktop entries use horizontal space to separate the date from the change and its supporting information. On mobile, that information becomes a vertical sequence within each card.</p>
        <p>The priority remains consistent. Identify the timing, understand the change, determine whether action is needed, and access further information.</p>
        <p>Compared with a multi-column swimlane, the agenda can follow a single reading direction on a narrow screen. The compromise is additional vertical length, particularly for entries with several actions.</p>
        <p>The design therefore needs a consistent hierarchy so the reader can distinguish the main message from supporting detail.</p>

    <CampaignSection id="outcome" toc="Outcome" title="An agenda-led direction, selected through comparison">
      <Copy>
        <p>The outcome was stakeholder agreement on the agenda direction for the experience under review.</p>
        <p>The broader brief began with a reusable calendar or timeline pattern. The exploration helped distinguish the different representations that could support that ambition, rather than assuming a timeline would suit every content distribution.</p>
        <p>For the selected Trust Calendar direction, the agenda addressed the central concern. Relevant changes could remain visible without empty months or years dominating the composition.</p>
        <p>This outcome reflects a design decision and stakeholder alignment. Implementation status and post-launch results are separate details to add when confirmed.</p>
        <p>The Agenda and Grid controls on the selected screen are part of that design. Their presence does not mean every calendar or grid candidate was approved, removed, or implemented.</p>
      </Copy>

    <CampaignSection id="reflection" toc="Reflection" title="Reusability includes knowing when a pattern stops helping">
      <Copy>
        <p>I began by exploring the timeline direction emphasized in the early discussions.</p>
        <p>As the work developed, the question shifted from how to make that structure work to which structure best supported the information we needed to present.</p>
        <p>The timeline remained useful for understanding shared intervals. Its limitations became clearer when milestones were sparse or unevenly distributed.</p>
        <p>Preparing alternatives allowed the team to evaluate those limitations directly. The agenda gave up proportional spacing in exchange for a more focused presentation of relevant entries.</p>
        <p>My main takeaway was to examine the content distribution as closely as the individual component. A structure can look appropriate with a full set of sample events and behave very differently when real gaps appear.</p>
        <p>Future evaluation would examine whether readers can identify the next relevant change, distinguish action-required from no-action entries, and understand the relationship between the dates shown.</p>
      </Copy>
